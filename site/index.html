Time to run 10 million policies and scenarios of a savings model

On this scale each grid line is ten times shorter than the last. We measured on one desktop-sized computer, described near the end.

The headline. Written to handle one policy at a time, this run takes 18 days. As lifelib supplies it, it takes 47 minutes. Our fast version takes 3 seconds on an ordinary processor and gives identical results. On a graphics processor it takes a tenth of a second, and the results differ in the later digits.

The rest of the deck explains where each gain comes from, what it takes to achieve, and what evidence it needs.

The question

Can our models run much faster and still be trusted?

Why speed is wanted
  • Simulations inside simulations. Some calculations run a full set of scenarios inside every scenario of another set. A thousand scenarios inside each of a thousand means a million projections.
  • More scenarios. A 1-in-200-year loss is estimated more reliably from more scenarios.
  • Answers during the meeting. Pricing and planning questions answered in seconds, not overnight.
  • Lower computing costs for existing runs.
Why trust is the hard part
  • Models are validated, signed off and audited. A faster model is a different program. The validation does not carry over to it automatically.
  • Matching totals proves little. Later we plant errors on purpose and show that checking totals misses about a third of them.
  • Some ways of going faster change the arithmetic. The answers then no longer match to the last digit, so "it matches" cannot be the test.
Commercial platforms already run actuarial models on graphics processors. This project looks at each level of speed in turn. For each one it describes what changes in the model and what evidence you need to rely on the results.

Both halves matter. Speed without evidence gives you a new, unvalidated model. Evidence without speed is where most teams are today.

Being realistic

Does an insurer need models this fast?

Often not. Many runs already finish overnight. We tested every level, from the simplest to the most extreme, so that each team can see where it would stop.

For sceptics: we do not claim every insurer needs a model 100,000 times faster. We went to the far end on purpose, to map the trade-offs. For most work we recommend the middle of the range.

01 · What a cashflow model does

Projecting one policy

A projection follows a policy forward one month at a time. Each month it works out how many policies are still in force. It then works out the cashflows: premiums coming in, and claims, surrenders and expenses going out.

Each month starts where the last one finished. The policies in force at the start of month 13 are those left at the end of month 12. This chain makes projections hard to speed up, and it comes up again later.

Finally, the model discounts the cashflows and adds them up. This gives a present value: one number for each policy in each scenario.

02 · Where the time goes

Four ways to run the same calculation

months →↓ policies

Click through the four tabs. Each square is one policy in one month. The colour shows where the computer is working at each moment.

The main point: a computer can work on many policies at once, because policies do not affect each other. It cannot work on many months at once, because each month needs the month before.

02 · Where the time goes

What can run in parallel

Across policies and scenarios

Yes, freely

Policies do not affect each other, so a computer can work on a million of them at once. Every speed-up in this deck relies on this.

Across months

No

Month 13 needs the policies in force and the account value at the end of month 12. However you arrange the work, each policy's months come one after another.

Inside one month

A little

A computer can work out premiums and claims for the same month side by side. But there are only a few dozen such amounts, which is not enough to keep thousands of processors busy.

Vectorising is the usual advice for speeding up Python models. It works out one month for all policies, then the next month for all policies. This avoids handling one number at a time. But it keeps every month's values for every policy in memory, and it keeps calculating policies that have already matured. Compiling turns this round. The model is translated into the processor's own instructions, and each policy runs through all its months, passing only a few numbers on to the next month.

04 · Evidence 1 · Compare every number

Results of the comparison

For each test portfolio, we run the original model unchanged and record all its results: policies in force, account values and each cashflow, for every policy and every month. The fast model's values must be identical.

We compare every value, not only totals, for two reasons. Errors can cancel out and hide in a total. And a difference in a single value points straight at the line of the model that is wrong.

The test portfolios include each model's own sample policies and a set of unusual cases. These include policies not yet started or already matured, ages at the ends of the mortality table, zero or extreme amounts, and cases where the original model itself gives an undefined result.

04 · Evidence 2 · Test the test

Testing the comparison with planted errors

To measure how good a check is, we planted errors in the fast model, one at a time. They were common kinds of error: a plus instead of a minus, a wrong constant, the wrong row of a table, the wrong variable, or a comparison that is out by one. We then ran each check against each planted error.

Comparing portfolio totals, or even each policy's present values, catches only about two thirds of them. Many errors affect amounts that present values never use, such as margins, account values and policy counts. Others cancel out.

Comparing every value catches 95% to 99%. The proofs in section 05 deal with almost all of the rest: they either reject them or show that they cannot change any result.

04 · Is it still the same model?

Limits of testing

Problem 1

Untested policies

A comparison says nothing about a policy that is not in the portfolio, such as an age, term or duration that nobody thought to include.

Problem 2

Rearranged sums

Stopping early, or rearranging the sums so they run faster, adds arithmetic that is not in the original. Results can differ in the last digit, so "identical" is no longer the test.

Problem 3

Different hardware

A graphics processor may combine a multiplication and an addition into one operation, and at 7 digits it keeps fewer digits. Results differ by design.

For all three problems, you need a statement about every policy, not a sample. A proof gives you that. For the third, you also need to know how far rounding can move the answer. Section 06 covers this.
05 · Proof, from first principles

Tests and proofs

Testing
Is 3 × (4 + 5) = 3 × 4 + 3 × 5 ?
27 = 27 ✓a = 2, b = 7, c = 1 ✓a = −1, b = 0.5, c = 8 ✓

Each check convinces you about that case. No number of checks covers every case.

Proof
a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c
for every number a, b and c

Shown once, from the rules of arithmetic, this is true for all numbers, including ones nobody has tried.

The same idea works for models. Instead of running the original and the fast model on numbers, run them on symbols, such as "the policy's age", "the sum assured" and "the mortality rate at this age". Each model then produces a formula, not a number. The question becomes: are the two formulas always equal?

The rest of this section depends on this one idea. Anyone comfortable with algebra already understands it: it is algebra done by a program.

05 · Proof, from first principles

Running both models on symbols

The original model, as written 
def claims(t):
    return claim_pp(t) * pols_death(t)

def pols_death(t):
    return pols_if_at(t, "BEF_DECR") * mort_rate_mth(t)
both become
sum assured × policies in force × monthly mortality(age, duration)
so they are equal for every policy
The fast model 
p_death = p_decr * q
claims  = sa * p_death

The program reads the original model's own code and runs it on symbols, so nobody has to retype the model. Some parts cannot be run on symbols, such as reading a value from a table or drawing random numbers. A written assumption replaces each such part, and we test that assumption separately by trying every position in the table.

A real example from the term assurance model, slightly simplified. The proof covers every result of the model, not just claims: between 20 and 77 results for each model.

05 · Proof, from first principles

Proving every month

Part 1 · The first month

Prove that in month 0 every value of the fast model equals the original's, and that it passes the right numbers on to month 1.

Part 2 · Any month, given the month before

Prove that if the numbers passed into a month are right, every value in that month is right, and it passes on the right numbers too.

Together these cover month 1, then month 2, and so on to any month. The first domino falls, and each one knocks over the next. Two proofs replace an endless number of tests. The same argument shows that stopping early is safe: once no policies remain, every later cashflow is zero.

Press → three times. Mathematicians call this "proof by induction". It lets us prove that every month of a projection of any length is right, without checking the months one by one.

05 · What a theorem prover is

Z3 and Lean

Theorem prover · Z3

Finds proofs and counterexamples

Z3 comes from Microsoft Research. You give it a statement, such as "for every policy, the two formulas are equal", and it searches for a proof. If the statement is false, it returns a counterexample: a specific policy, with its age, term and amounts, on which the two models disagree.

It is fast and clever. It is also large: hundreds of thousands of lines of code that you have to trust.

Proof assistant · Lean

Checks every step of a proof

Mathematicians use Lean to check research proofs line by line. It accepts a statement only when every step of the proof follows from the rules. The part that does the checking is small, and many people have studied it closely.

It is slower and stricter. Because of it, nobody has to take Z3's word for the result.

Think of Z3 as a gifted student who solves the problem quickly, and Lean as the examiner who checks every line of the working. When both accept a result, you rely on the examiner, not the student.

05 · Proof

Why Lean matters at levels 4 to 6

Levels 2 to 3

Comparison

The fast model reproduces the original exactly. Proof adds "for every policy", but a reviewer can rely on the comparison alone.

Level 4

Proof

Stopping early and rearranging sums change the arithmetic. The claim that results are still the same comes from the proof, so the proof must be trustworthy.

Levels 5 to 6

Proof and error bounds

The proof says the calculation is the same. The error bound says how far rounding moved the answer. Neither is enough alone.

This is where a separate proof checker earns its place. A proof found by a large automated tool is only as good as that tool. Because Lean checks every statement again, the case for the fastest versions rests on Lean's small, much-examined checker.

A caveat, repeated on the limits slide. Our own code and a short written description of the model produce the statements, and Lean checks them. Someone has to review both the code and the description.

06 · Rounding

How computers round numbers

Proofs deal with exact numbers. Computers store a fixed number of digits: about 16 in double precision, the normal choice for financial work, and about 7 in single precision, which graphics processors handle many times faster.

Every sum rounds its result. Usually the effect is tiny. It becomes large when you subtract one big number from another to get a small one. Net cashflow is an example: premiums minus claims minus the change in account value. The leading digits cancel, and what is left is mostly rounding error.

06 · How far rounding can move an answer

Interval arithmetic

We run the fast model once more, this time carrying every number as a pair: one known to be below the exact value, and one above. Every sum rounds the lower number down and the upper number up, so the exact answer cannot escape. When the pair cannot settle a comparison, the program keeps both outcomes. This is called interval arithmetic.

The result for each policy is a narrow range that must contain the exact answer. You can then measure any computed answer, from any device, against it. You do not need to know how that device rounds.

Press → twice. The positions are for illustration, not to scale. The range is typically about a millionth of a millionth of the value wide. Results at 7 digits fall well outside it. Their error is at most the distance to the far end of the range.

06 · How far rounding can move an answer · results

Maximum rounding error by model

The largest possible error over every policy in every test portfolio (69,200 rows), as a share of the value. A figure of 1e-7 means one part in ten million.

At 16 digits, the graphics processor is as close to exact as the original model. The first two columns are the same. What remains is rounding that the original model does as well.
At 7 digits, one policy's net cashflow can move by 1% or 2% in the savings models, where large account values cancel out. The effect is far smaller for term and traditional business, and portfolio totals stay within a few parts in ten million.
07 · Choosing a level

Choosing a level

Is run time a problem?No: change nothing, or vectorise (level 1). Speed is not free.
Must every figure match the current model exactly?Yes: compile, and use all processor cores (levels 2 and 3). On our tests this ran 100 to 420 times faster than lifelib's version, with identical results, ordinary hardware, and evidence anyone can follow.
Still not fast enough?Restructure (level 4): stop early and rearrange sums. This is up to about twice as fast again. Results are equal in exact arithmetic, proved for every policy.
Millions of projections, with results used only in total?Use a graphics processor (levels 5 and 6). Use 16 digits if the device is built for it. Use 7 digits only where the worst possible error is small compared with the uncertainty already in the simulation.

We recommend levels 1 to 3 by default. They give most of the speed with the simplest evidence. Levels 4 to 6 are for work like the last row of the "does an insurer need this" table.

08 · Comparison with other platforms

What is new

01

The original model is the reference

Nobody replaces or rewrites the specification of the existing model. It runs unchanged, and the fast version is checked against it.

02

Every value is compared

The fast model must agree on every amount in every month, and we measure how strong that check is.

03

Proof for every policy

The models are proved equal for every possible policy and month, including the shortcuts that make the fast versions fast.

04

Proofs checked separately

Lean checks every proof again, so the case does not rest on the tool that found it.

05

Guaranteed error bounds

For runs on a graphics processor, each policy's possible error has a guaranteed limit. It is not an estimate.

06

The converter is not trusted

The checks do not trust the converter. So an AI coding agent can do the conversion, following a written procedure, and the checks decide whether to accept it.

We do not know how commercial platforms work inside, and some may do parts of this. We have not seen the combination elsewhere, or seen it done openly on public models.

08 · The models we used

Models and computer used

lifelib · CashValue_ME

Savings

A universal life savings product. An account value earns random investment returns and pays fees and a cost of insurance. Death and maturity benefits are guaranteed. Monthly, up to 95 years. 79 formulas.

As above, but more people lapse when the guarantee is worth little. Five combinations of fees, mortality and lapses, each proved separately. 92 formulas.

Level term assurance, monthly, with premiums from a rate table. It also works out its own net premium. 44 formulas.

lifelib · fastlife

Traditional life, annual

Term, whole life and endowment, with net premium reserves from commutation columns, and cash values. Four modules. Annual, 103 years.

lifelib is a free library of actuarial models written in Python, for teaching and research. Its models are smaller and simpler than real production models. Because they are public, anyone can repeat every figure here.

Computer: one NVIDIA DGX Spark, a desktop-sized computer with 20 processor cores and a GB10 graphics processor that share 128 GB of memory. All times come from one run, with nothing else running.

08 · Limits

Limitations

  • These are teaching models. Production models have management actions, many more products, and policies that affect each other. Nothing here shows the effort or the gains for those.
  • The proofs are about a description of the original model. Written assumptions replace the parts that cannot be run on symbols. We test these assumptions but do not prove them. In one model, a person wrote the reserve formula by hand. Someone must review the description like any other model documentation.
  • The checks are designed to catch honest mistakes by a person or an AI agent. They do not protect against someone who deliberately tries to get a wrong model accepted.
  • Preparing the inputs, such as reading model points and building tables: we check this by comparison, but do not prove it.
  • Error bounds apply to each policy, after the run. They say how far each answer can be from the exact one. They say nothing about policies that were not run.
  • Timings come from one computer. Graphics processors vary widely in how fast they do 16-digit sums.
