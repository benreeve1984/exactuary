One run: 10 million policy-scenarios of a savings model

Bar length on a logarithmic scale. Measured on one desktop-sized machine; see "The models we used".

The headline: a run that would take 18 days written one policy at a time, and 47 minutes in its vectorised form, takes 3 seconds on an ordinary processor with results identical to the original, and a tenth of a second on a graphics processor with results that differ in the later digits.

The rest of the deck explains where each step of that comes from, what each one costs, and what evidence each needs before you would rely on it.

The question

"Could our models run 1,000 times faster? And would we trust them if they did?"

Why speed is wanted
  • Stochastic within stochastic. Some calculations need a full simulation inside every scenario of another simulation: a thousand times a thousand projections.
  • More scenarios. Tail measures such as a 1-in-200 loss are estimated more steadily from more scenarios.
  • Questions answered while they are being asked. Pricing and planning what-ifs in seconds rather than overnight.
  • A smaller computing bill for the runs already being made.
Why trust is the hard part
  • Models are validated, signed off and audited. A faster model is a different program, and the validation does not carry over automatically.
  • Reconciling totals is weak evidence. We show later that it misses about a third of deliberately introduced errors.
  • Some ways of going faster change the arithmetic itself, so the answers no longer match to the last digit. Then "it matches" cannot be the test.
Commercial platforms already offer to run actuarial models on graphics processors. This project asks a narrower question: for each step of speed, what exactly changes, and what evidence would let you rely on the result?

Both halves matter. Speed without evidence is a new, unvalidated model. Evidence without speed is where most teams already are.

Being realistic

Does an insurer need models this fast?

Often not. Many runs already fit comfortably into an overnight window. This was a test of the whole range, from the simplest step to the most extreme, so that each team can see where it would stop.

Most of the gain comes from compiling the model and using every processor core: between about 100 and 420 times the vectorised model on our monthly test models, with identical results and no special hardware. Stopping projections early roughly doubles that again where policies mature. The last steps are for the last row of this table.

The point to make to sceptics: we are not claiming every insurer needs a hundred-thousand-fold speed-up. We deliberately went to the far end to map the trade-offs, and the recommendation for most work is the middle of the range.

01 · What a cashflow model does

One policy, one month at a time

A projection follows a policy forward month by month. Each month it works out how many policies are still in force, then the cashflows: premiums in; death claims, surrenders and expenses out.

Each month starts from where the last one finished. The policies in force at the start of month 13 are those left at the end of month 12. This chain is what makes projections hard to speed up, and it comes up again and again.

At the end the cashflows are discounted and added up to give present values: one number per policy per scenario.

The same arithmetic, four ways of doing it

months →↓ policies

The key fact for a layman: you can work on many policies at once, because policies do not affect each other. You cannot work on many months at once, because each month needs the month before.

02 · Where the time goes

What can be done in parallel, and what cannot

Across policies and scenarios

Yes, freely

Policies do not affect each other, so a million of them can be worked on at once. This is what vectorising, multi-core processors and graphics processors all exploit.

Across months

No

Month 13 needs the policies in force and the account value at the end of month 12. However the work is arranged, each policy's months happen one after another.

Inside one month

A little

Premiums and claims for the same month can be computed side by side, but there are only a few dozen such quantities: not enough to keep thousands of processing units busy.

Vectorising (the usual advice to speed up Python models) processes all policies for one month, then all policies for the next month. It removes the cost of handling one number at a time, but it keeps every month's values for every policy in memory, and it cannot stop early for a policy that has already matured. Compiling turns this around: each policy runs through all its months in machine code, carrying forward only the few numbers the next month needs.

04 · Evidence 1 · Compare every number

1.6 billion values compared. None differs.

For each test portfolio the original model is run unchanged and every intermediate value is recorded: policies in force, account values, each cashflow, for every policy and every month. The fast model's values must be identical, not merely close.

Comparing intermediate values rather than totals matters because errors cancel and hide in totals, and because an intermediate difference points straight at the line that is wrong.

Portfolios include the model's own sample and an "edge" portfolio generated to reach unusual cases: policies not yet started, already matured, at the ends of the mortality table, with zero or extreme amounts, and cases where the original itself produces an undefined result.

04 · Evidence 2 · Test the test

Reconciling totals misses a third of errors

To measure how good a check is, we planted errors in the fast model one at a time, of the kinds people make: a plus for a minus, a wrong constant, a wrong table row, the wrong variable, a comparison off by one. Each check was then run on each planted error.

Comparing portfolio totals, or even each policy's present values, catches only about two thirds. Many errors sit in quantities that present values never touch (margins, account values, policy counts), or cancel out.

Comparing every intermediate value catches 95% to 99%. Almost all of the few that survive are either refuted by the proofs in section 05 or shown to be harmless: edits that cannot change any result.

04 · The limit of testing

Testing covers the policies you tried

Problem 1

Untested policies

A comparison says nothing about a policy that is not in the portfolio: an age, a term or a duration nobody thought to include.

Problem 2

Restructured calculations

Stopping early, or regrouping the algebra so it runs faster, does arithmetic the original never did. The results can differ in the last digit, so "identical" is no longer the test.

Problem 3

Different hardware

A graphics processor may combine a multiplication and an addition into one step, and single precision keeps fewer digits. Results differ by design.

For all three we need a statement about every policy, not a sample: that is what a proof gives. And for the third we also need to know how far the rounding can move the answer: that is the guaranteed interval in section 06.
05 · Proof, from first principles

A test checks examples. A proof covers every case.

Testing
Is 3 × (4 + 5) = 3 × 4 + 3 × 5 ?
27 = 27 ✓a = 2, b = 7, c = 1 ✓a = −1, b = 0.5, c = 8 ✓

Each check is convincing for that case. No number of checks covers every case.

Proof
a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c
for every number a, b and c

Shown once, from the rules of arithmetic, it holds for all values, including ones nobody has tried.

The same idea applies to models. Instead of running the original and the fast model on numbers, run them on symbols: "the policy's age", "the sum assured", "the mortality rate at this age". Each model then produces a formula rather than a number, and the question becomes whether the two formulas are always equal.

This is the one idea the rest of the section depends on. If the audience is comfortable with algebra, they already understand symbolic execution: it is algebra done by a program.

05 · Proof, from first principles

Running both models on symbols

The original, as written 
def claims(t):
    return claim_pp(t) * pols_death(t)

def pols_death(t):
    return pols_if_at(t, "BEF_DECR") * mort_rate_mth(t)
both become
sum assured × policies in force × monthly mortality(age, duration)
so they are equal for every policy
The fast model 
p_death = p_decr * q
claims  = sa * p_death

The original's own source code is read and run on symbols, so it is not re-typed by hand. Where it uses something a symbolic run cannot follow (reading a table from a data frame, drawing random numbers), that piece is replaced by a stated assumption, and the assumption is tested separately by trying every table position.

Real example from the term assurance model, slightly simplified. The proof is done for every output of the model, not just claims: 20 to 77 outputs per model.

05 · Proof, from first principles

Every month, by induction

Step 1 · The first month

Prove that in month 0 every output of the fast model equals the original's, and that it carries forward the right numbers.

Step 2 · Any month from the one before

Prove that if the numbers carried into month t are right, then every output in month t is right and the numbers carried out are right again.

Together these cover month 1, then month 2, and so on to any month: the first domino falls, and each knocks over the next. Two proofs replace an infinite number of tests. The same argument shows that stopping early is safe: once no policies remain, every later cashflow is zero.

Press → three times. This is the classical principle of mathematical induction. It is why we can prove equality for every month of a projection of any length without checking them one by one.

05 · What a theorem prover is

Two kinds of tool: one finds proofs, one checks them

Automated prover · Z3

Finds a proof, or a counterexample

Z3, from Microsoft Research, is given a statement such as "for every policy, the two formulas are equal" and searches for a proof. If the statement is false it returns a counterexample: a specific policy, with its age, term and amounts, on which the two models disagree.

It is fast and clever, and large: hundreds of thousands of lines of code that must be trusted to be right.

Proof assistant · Lean

Checks every step of a proof

Lean, used by mathematicians to check research proofs line by line, accepts a statement only when every step of its proof follows from the rules. The part that does the checking, the "kernel", is small enough to have been studied closely by many people.

It is slower and more demanding, and it is the reason we do not have to take Z3's word for it.

An analogy: Z3 is a gifted student who solves the problem quickly. Lean is the examiner who checks every line of the working. A result both accept rests on the examiner, not on the student.
05 · What proof catches

Errors no portfolio would have shown

Example 1 · Regrouping a constant

To speed up the savings model, account × (1 − 0.01 / 12) was rewritten with the constant worked out in advance as 0.99916666….

Refuted. The decimal written down is not exactly the same number as 1 − 0.01/12, so the two models are not equal for every policy. A comparison would show a difference only in the last digits, which any tolerance would hide.
Example 2 · The wrong table row

A planted error read initial commission from the next product's row of the expense table.

Refuted, with a counterexample policy. Comparison caught this one too, because neighbouring products have different commission rates. Where neighbouring rows happen to agree on every test policy, only the proof can tell the right row from the wrong one.

05 · Why Lean matters when you push hardest

Beyond step 3, the proof is the evidence

Steps 2–3

Comparison carries the weight

The fast model reproduces the original exactly. Proof adds "for every policy", but a reviewer can rely on the comparison alone.

Step 4

Proof carries the weight

Stopping early and regrouping algebra change the arithmetic. The claim that results are still the same comes from the proof, so the proof must be trustworthy.

Steps 5–6

Proof and bounds together

The proof says the calculation is the same; the guaranteed interval says how far rounding moved the answer. Neither alone is enough.

This is where an independent proof checker earns its place. A proof found by a large automated tool is only as good as that tool. Re-checking every statement in Lean means the argument for the most aggressive versions rests on a small, widely scrutinised checker, not on the software that found the proof.

The honest caveat, in the limits slide: Lean checks the statements it is given. Turning the model into those statements is done by our own code and by a short written description of the model, and both have to be reviewed.

06 · Rounding

Computers keep 16 digits, or 7

Proofs are about exact numbers. Computers store a fixed number of digits: about 16 in double precision, the normal choice for financial work, and about 7 in single precision, which graphics processors handle many times faster.

Each operation rounds its result. Usually the effect is tiny. It becomes large when two big numbers are subtracted to give a small one, as when net cashflow is premiums minus claims minus the change in account value: the leading digits cancel and the rounding errors are what is left.

06 · Guaranteed error bounds

An interval that must contain the exact answer

The fast model is run once more in interval arithmetic: every number is carried as a pair, one known to be below the exact value and one above. Each operation rounds the lower number down and the upper one up, so the exact answer can never escape. Where the pair cannot decide a comparison, both outcomes are kept.

The result for each policy is a narrow interval that is guaranteed to contain the answer exact arithmetic would give. Any computed answer, from any device, can then be measured against it, without needing to know how that device rounds.

Press → twice. Illustrative positions, not to scale. The interval for double precision is typically 10-12 of the value wide; single-precision results fall well outside it, and the distance to the far end of the interval is a guaranteed bound on their error.

06 · Guaranteed error bounds · results

How far each version can be from the exact answer

Greatest distance over every policy of every test portfolio (69,200 rows, all certified), relative to the size of the value. A figure of 1e-7 means one part in ten million.

Double precision on the graphics processor is as close to exact as the original model is. The figures in the first two columns are the same: what remains is the rounding the original also does.
Single precision can move an individual policy's net cashflow by one or two per cent in the savings models, where large account values cancel. It is far smaller for term and traditional business, and portfolio totals stay within a few parts in ten million.
07 · Choosing a level

Which step to stop at

Is run time actually a problem?No → change nothing, or vectorise (step 1). Speed is not free.
Must every figure match the current model exactly?Yes → compiled, on all processor cores (steps 2–3). Between about 100 and 420 times the vectorised model on our tests, identical results, ordinary hardware, evidence anyone can follow.
Still not fast enough?→ Restructure (step 4): stop early, regroup algebra. Up to about twice as fast again; results equal in exact arithmetic, proved for every policy.
Millions of projections, results used in aggregate?→ Graphics processor. Double precision if the device is built for it; single precision only where the measured, guaranteed error is small against the uncertainty that is already in the simulation.

Our default recommendation: steps 1 to 3. They give the bulk of the speed with the simplest evidence. Steps 4 to 6 are for workloads in the last row of the "does an insurer need this" table.

08 · What is new here

Compared with platforms that run models on GPUs

01

The original stays the reference

The existing model is not replaced or re-specified. It is run unchanged, and the fast version is checked against it.

02

Every intermediate value, not totals

Agreement is shown for every quantity in every month, and the strength of that check is itself measured.

03

Proof for every policy

Equivalence is proved for all possible policies and months, including the shortcuts that make the fast versions fast.

04

Checked by an independent kernel

Every proof is re-checked in Lean, so the argument does not rest on the tool that found it.

05

Guaranteed error bounds

For GPU and single-precision runs, each policy's error is bounded, not estimated.

06

Built for an untrusted converter

Because nothing the converter writes is taken on trust, the conversion can be done by an AI coding agent following a written procedure, with the checks deciding.

We do not know the internals of commercial platforms, and some may do parts of this. The combination, and doing it in the open on public models, is what we have not seen elsewhere.

08 · The models we used

Four public models, one machine

lifelib · CashValue_ME

Savings

Universal-life savings product: account value with stochastic investment returns, fees, cost of insurance, guaranteed death and maturity benefits. Monthly, up to 95 years. 79 formulas.

lifelib · CashValue_ME_EX2

As above, with lapses that rise when the guarantee is worth little. Five configurations of fees, mortality and lapses, each proved separately. 92 formulas.

lifelib · BasicTerm_ME

Term assurance

Level term assurance, monthly, premiums from a rate table; also prices its own net premium. 44 formulas.

lifelib · fastlife

Traditional life, annual

Term, whole life and endowment; net-premium reserves from commutation columns; cash values; four modules. Annual, 103 years.

lifelib is an open-source library of actuarial models in Python, maintained for teaching and research. The models are smaller and more regular than production models; they are public, which lets anyone repeat every figure here.

Machine: one NVIDIA DGX Spark: a desktop-sized computer with 20 Arm processor cores and a GB10 graphics processor sharing 128 GB of memory. All times are from one sequential run on an otherwise idle machine.

08 · Limits

What this does not show

  • These are teaching models. Production models have management actions, many more products and interactions between policies. Nothing here shows the effort or the gains on those.
  • The proofs are about a description of the original. Pieces the symbolic run cannot follow are replaced by stated assumptions, which are tested but not proved; in one model the reserve formula is described by hand. The description must be reviewed like any other piece of model documentation.
  • The checks are designed to catch honest mistakes, by a person or an AI agent. They are not a defence against someone deliberately trying to pass a wrong model.
  • Preparing the inputs (reading model points, building tables) is checked by comparison, not proved.
  • Error bounds are per policy, after the run. They say how far each computed answer can be from exact; they do not promise anything for policies not run.
  • Timings are from one machine. Graphics processors in particular vary widely in double-precision speed.
Exactuary

Most of the speed needs no special hardware and keeps every number identical. The last steps need proof, and Lean is what makes that proof trustworthy.
