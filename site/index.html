The headline. Written to handle one policy at a time, this run takes 18 days. As lifelib supplies it, it takes 47 minutes. Compiled and run on 20 processor cores with early exit (level 4), it takes 3.2 seconds. On a graphics processor it takes a tenth of a second, and the results can differ materially for individual policies.

The rest of the deck explains where each gain comes from, what it takes to achieve, and how we check that the results match.

Background

Actuarial cashflow models

Life insurers use actuarial cashflow models to estimate what their policies will pay out and bring in over many years. The model takes each policy, such as a savings plan or a life cover, and projects its premiums, claims, surrenders and expenses month by month. It then discounts these future cashflows to a single value today.

Insurers use the results to set reserves, price new products, report under rules such as Solvency II and IFRS 17, and work out how much capital to hold. A full run covers every policy, often under hundreds or thousands of economic scenarios. Many teams write these models in Python or in specialist actuarial software.

The four models in this experiment

Savings

A savings policy with an account value, investment returns, fees, and guaranteed death and maturity benefits.

Savings with dynamic lapses

The same product, but more people cancel when the guarantee is worth little.

Term assurance

Life cover for a fixed term, with monthly premiums.

Traditional life, annual

Term, whole life and endowment policies, with reserves and cash values, projected year by year.

All four come from lifelib, an open source library of actuarial models written in Python. They are simpler than production models, but they use the same kinds of calculation.

This slide sets the scene for readers outside actuarial work. Everything later in the deck applies to these four models.

The question

Purpose

Why speed is wanted
  • Some calculations run a full set of scenarios inside every scenario of another set. 1,000 scenarios inside each of 1,000 scenarios means one million projections.
  • More scenarios reduce the sampling uncertainty in an estimate such as a 1-in-200-year loss.
  • Teams can answer pricing and planning questions in seconds.
  • Existing runs cost less to compute.
Why trust is the hard part
  • Teams validate and approve their models. A faster model is a different program, so it's not covered by the existing validation.
  • Matching final results is weak evidence. In our tests we compared the present values on a sample portfolio, both in total and for each policy. That missed 27% to 42% of deliberately planted errors. Comparing every value in every month caught 95% to 99%.
  • Some ways of going faster change the order or the precision of the arithmetic. This adds small rounding differences, or numerical noise, so the results no longer match exactly. We then need a guaranteed limit on how far rounding can move each result.
There are two main ways to make a model faster. The first is to rewrite it as optimised, lower-level code: compiled to run directly on the processor and spread across all its cores. The second is to move the calculation to an accelerator chip, such as a graphics processor (GPU). Commercial platforms already run actuarial models on graphics processors. This project looks at each level of speed in turn. For each level it describes what changes in the model and how we can prove that the optimised model matches the original.

An optimised model needs proof that it matches the model it replaces.

Being realistic

Is this speed needed?

Often not. Many runs already finish overnight. We tested every level so that each team can see the costs and gains before deciding where to stop.

Most of the gain comes from levels 2 and 3: compiling the model and using every processor core. On our three monthly test models, level 3 ran about 106 to 424 times as fast as lifelib's version, with no special hardware. Level 4 added a further factor of about two for three of the four models, and nothing for the dynamic lapse model.

We tested the most extreme levels to show their costs. For most work, levels 2 and 3 are enough.

01 · What a cashflow model does

Projecting one policy

A projection follows a policy forward one month at a time. Each month it works out how many policies are still in force. It then works out the cashflows: premiums coming in, and claims, surrenders and expenses going out.

Each month starts where the last one finished. The policies in force at the start of month 13 are those left at the end of month 12. This chain makes projections hard to speed up, and it comes up again later.

Finally, the model discounts the cashflows and adds them up. This gives a present value: one number for each policy in each scenario.

02 · Where the time goes

Four ways to run the same calculation

months →↓ policies

Click through the four tabs. Each square is one policy in one month. The colour shows where the computer is working at each moment.

The main point: a computer can work on many policies at once, because in these models policies do not affect each other. It cannot work on many months at once, because each month needs the month before.

02 · Where the time goes

What can run in parallel

Across policies and scenarios

Independent policies

In the example models, policies do not affect each other, so a computer can work on one million of them at once. Levels 3, 5 and 6 rely on this.

Across months

Sequential periods

Month 13 needs the policies in force and the account value at the end of month 12. However you arrange the work, each policy's months come one after another.

Inside one month

Calculations within a period

A computer can work out premiums and claims for the same month side by side. There are only a few dozen such amounts, which cannot keep thousands of processors busy.

Vectorising is common advice for speeding up Python models. It works out one month for all policies, then the next month for all policies. This avoids handling one number at a time. The lifelib versions also keep every month's values for every policy in memory, and keep calculating policies that have already matured. Compiling turns this round. The model is translated into the processor's own instructions, Each policy runs through all its months and passes a small set of values to the next month.

04 · Evidence 1 · Compare every number

Results of the comparison

For each test portfolio, we run the original model unchanged and record the selected values: policies in force, account values and each cashflow, for every policy and every period. The fast model's values must be identical. In the annual model, 20 cells fail in the original itself, so they are left out; that model's README lists them.

We compare individual values as well as totals, for two reasons. Errors can cancel out and hide in a total. And a difference in a single value points straight at the line of the model that is wrong.

The test portfolios include each model's own sample policies and a set of unusual cases. These include policies not yet started or already matured, ages at the ends of the mortality table, zero or extreme amounts, and cases where the original model itself gives an undefined result.

04 · Evidence 2 · Test the test

Testing the comparison with planted errors

To measure how good a check is, we planted errors in the fast model, one at a time. They were common kinds of error: a plus instead of a minus, a wrong constant, the wrong row of a table, the wrong variable, or a comparison that is out by one. We then ran each check against each planted error.

Comparing portfolio totals, or even each policy's present values, catches only 58% to 73% of them. Many errors affect amounts that present values never use, such as margins, account values and policy counts. Others cancel out.

Comparing every recorded value catches 95% to 99%. For most of the rest, the proofs in section 05 either find a difference or show that the change makes no difference in exact arithmetic.

04 · Is it still the same model?

Limits of testing

Problem 1

Untested policies

A comparison says nothing about a policy that is not in the portfolio, such as an age, term or duration that nobody thought to include.

Problem 2

Rearranged sums

Stopping early, or rearranging the sums so they run faster, adds arithmetic that is not in the original. Results can differ in the last digit, so exact matching is no longer the test.

Problem 3

Different hardware

A graphics processor may combine a multiplication and an addition into one operation, and at seven digits it keeps fewer digits. Results differ by design.

For all three problems, a statement about any policy helps, and a proof can give one under stated assumptions. For the second and third, you also need to know how far rounding can move the answer. Section 06 covers this.

Proof
a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c
for every number a, b and c

The rules of arithmetic establish this for all numbers, including ones nobody has tried.

The same idea works for models. Instead of running the original and the fast model on numbers, run them on symbols, such as "the policy's age", "the sum assured" and "the mortality rate at this age". Each model then produces an algebraic expression. The question becomes: are the two expressions always equal?

The rest of this section depends on this one idea. A program performs the algebra.

05 · Proof, from first principles

Running both models on symbols

The original model, as written 
def claims(t):
    return claim_pp(t) * pols_death(t)

def pols_death(t):
    return pols_if_at(t, "BEF_DECR") * mort_rate_mth(t)
both become

so they are equal for every policy
The fast model 
p_death = p_decr * q
claims  = sa * p_death

The program reads the supported formulas in the original model's own code and runs them on symbols. A separate written description supplies assumptions for the other parts, such as reading a value from a table or drawing random numbers. In the annual model it also includes a handwritten reserve formula. Each assumption needs its own tests and review: for tables, we try every position.

05 · Proof, from first principles

Proving every month

Part 1 · The first month

Prove that in month 0 every value of the fast model equals the original's, and that it passes the right numbers on to month 1.

Part 2 · Any month, given the month before

Prove that if the numbers passed into a month are right, every value in that month is right, and it passes on the right numbers too.

Together these cover month 1, then month 2, and so on to any month. The first domino falls, and each one knocks over the next. The same argument shows that stopping early is safe in exact arithmetic: once no policies remain, every later cashflow is zero. Computer results can still differ where the original later produces an undefined value; in the dynamic lapses stress test this affects 20 rows.

Press → three times. Mathematicians call this "proof by induction". A general result in Lean carries the two parts to every month; connecting each model to that result is a step a reviewer must check.

05 · What a theorem prover is

Z3 and Lean

Theorem prover · Z3

Finds proofs and counterexamples

Z3 comes from Microsoft Research. You give it a statement, such as "for every policy, the two formulas are equal", and it searches for a proof. If the statement is false, it returns a counterexample: specific values, such as an age, term and amounts, on which the two models disagree. It can also report that it cannot decide.

It is a large program, and its verdict depends on that program being correct.

Proof assistant · Lean

Checks every step of a proof

Mathematicians use Lean to check research proofs line by line. It accepts a statement only when every step of the proof follows from the rules. The part that does the checking is small, and many people have studied it closely.

Exactuary exports each statement to Lean, which checks its own proof of it. This removes the reliance on Z3's verdict. Z3 works with real numbers here; Lean works with fractions.

Think of Z3 as a gifted student who solves the problem quickly, and Lean as the examiner who checks every line of the working. When both accept a result, the examiner's check is the one to rely on.

Example 1 · Working out a constant in advance

To speed up the savings model, we rewrote account × (1 − 0.01 / 12) with the constant worked out in advance as 0.99916666….

Z3 rejects this change. The written decimal is not exactly the same number as 1 − 0.01/12, so the two models are not equal for every policy. A comparison would show a difference only in the last digits, and a tolerance wider than that difference would accept the change.
Example 2 · The wrong table row

A planted error read the initial commission from the next product's row of the expense table.

Z3 rejects this change and gives a counterexample policy. Comparison caught it too, because neighbouring products have different commission rates. The proof finds the wrong row even where the tested table values happen to agree.

05 · Proof

Why Lean matters at levels 4 to 6

Levels 2 to 3

Comparison

The fast model reproduces the original's recorded values on the test portfolios. Proof extends this to any policy under stated assumptions, but a reviewer can rely on the comparison.

Level 4

Proof

Stopping early and rearranging sums change the arithmetic. The claim that results agree in exact arithmetic comes from the proof, and separate checks measure the effect of rounding.

Levels 5 to 6

Proof and error bounds

The proof covers the calculation in exact arithmetic. The error bound says how far rounding can have moved the answer. Both are needed.

At these levels a separate proof checker matters. A large automated tool finds the proofs; Lean then checks a separate proof of each statement with a small, widely examined checking program. The translation into Lean, the assumptions and the connection to the full projection still need review.

A caveat, repeated on the limits slide. Our own code and a short written description of the model produce the statements, and Lean checks them. Someone has to review both the code and the description.

06 · Rounding

How computers round numbers

Proofs deal with exact numbers. Computers store a fixed number of digits: about 16 in double precision, the normal choice for financial work, and about seven in single precision, which graphics processors handle many times faster.

Computer arithmetic rounds results to fit the available digits. Usually the effect is tiny. When large amounts nearly cancel, a small error in either amount can become a large share of the difference. Net cashflow can be sensitive for this reason.

06 · How far rounding can move an answer

Interval arithmetic

We run the fast model once more, this time carrying every number as a pair: one known to be below the exact value, and one above. Every sum rounds the lower number down and the upper number up, so the exact answer cannot escape. When the pair cannot settle a comparison, the program keeps both outcomes. This method is interval arithmetic.

The result for each row in the checking run is a narrow range. It contains the exact answer for the prepared inputs, provided the interval program is correct. You can then measure any computed answer in seven or 16 digits, from any device, against it. The check reports any row it cannot bound.

Illustrative positions, not to scale. A computed result can lie outside the range; its distance to the far end bounds its error.

Press → twice. The positions are for illustration, not to scale. The range is typically about a millionth of a millionth of the value wide. Results at seven digits fall well outside it. Their error is at most the distance to the far end of the range.

07 · Choosing a level

Choosing a level

Is run time a problem?No: change nothing, or vectorise (level 1). Speed is not free.
Must every figure match the current model exactly?Yes: compile, and use all processor cores (levels 2 and 3). On the three monthly tests this ran about 106 to 424 times as fast as lifelib's version, on ordinary hardware. Recorded values match on the test portfolios; present values match when added in the original order.
Still not fast enough?Restructure (level 4): stop early and rearrange sums. This is up to about twice as fast again. A proof shows equality in exact arithmetic under stated assumptions; rounding checks cover the rest.
Millions of projections, with results used only in total?Use a graphics processor (levels 5 and 6). Use 16 digits if the device is built for it. For seven digits, set an acceptable error for each reported output and check it on the intended workload, including totals, percentiles and ratios.

We recommend levels 1 to 3 by default. They give most of the speed with the simplest evidence. Levels 4 to 6 are for work like the last row of the "does an insurer need this" table.

08 · Comparison with other platforms

What is new

01

The original model is the reference

The existing model runs unchanged, and the checks compare the fast version with it.

02

Individual values are compared

The fast model must agree on each recorded amount in each period, and planted errors measure how strong that check is.

03

Proof for every policy

Proofs show equality in exact arithmetic for any policy under stated assumptions, including the shortcuts that make the fast versions fast.

04

Proofs checked separately

Lean checks a separate proof of each statement, which removes the reliance on Z3.

05

Guaranteed error bounds

A separate check bounds each row's rounding error for runs on a graphics processor.

06

Built for AI-assisted conversion

A person or an AI coding agent can write the fast model, following a written procedure. The checks assess the result. A reviewer still checks the written description and assumptions.

We do not know how commercial platforms work inside, and some may do parts of this.

08 · Limits

Limitations

  • These are open source lifelib models, not production models. Production models have management actions, many more products, and policies that affect each other. Nothing here shows the effort or the gains for those.
  • The proofs concern a written description of the original model. Written assumptions replace the parts that cannot be run on symbols. We test these assumptions but do not prove them. In one model, a person wrote the reserve formula by hand. Someone must review the description like any other model documentation.
  • The checks aim to detect honest mistakes during conversion, by a person or an AI agent. They do not protect against someone who deliberately tries to get a wrong model accepted. Other conversion errors may remain.
  • Input preparation, such as reading model points and building tables, has comparison tests but no proof.
  • Error bounds apply to the rows in a checking run, after the run. They say nothing about rows that were not checked.
  • Timings come from one computer and measure the projection only. Graphics processors vary widely in how fast they do 16-digit sums.
Exactuary

Choose the lowest level that meets the run-time requirement and the agreed accuracy standard. Review the comparisons, proof assumptions and rounding bounds for the intended workload.
